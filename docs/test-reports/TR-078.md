## Part 1 — The measurement that retired TR-077's own objection

TR-077 declined to build the streamer on the reasoning that correct multi-tile virtual texturing
needs a tile atlas **plus an indirection lookup**, and that the half-measures look like rendering
defects. The first half of that was right. The second half turned out to be avoidable, and the
thing that retired it was a measurement rather than an argument.

The visible tiles always form a **contiguous rectangle** in the tile grid — worst case **3×3 at
level 3 and 4×4 at level 4**, checked across 400 sub-camera positions. That follows from the patch
being a single connected region of a sphere, and it collapses the design:

> A contiguous rectangle needs no indirection table. Compose those tiles into ONE texture, hand the
> shader the UV rect that atlas covers, and the lookup is a subtract-and-divide.

So `planet-vt-stream.ts` is short, and it is short because the problem got smaller, not because
corners were cut. TR-077's stop was correct on the information it had; this is what changed.

**Everything in the streamer is failure-tolerant by construction**, which is what lets it run
speculatively on arrival. The sphere is already on screen and already correct before any tile is
fetched; detail is strictly additive. A missing tile leaves that patch of the atlas filled with the
neutral normal (128,128,255) — flat, not a violent normal flip. A failed atlas leaves `uHasDetail`
at 0. A device that cannot give a 2D context gets no detail rather than an exception. Nothing here
can make a working body worse.

Two details worth naming:

- **`rectChanged` is what stops a per-frame rebuild.** The sub-camera point drifts continuously as
  a body rotates, so without it the streamer would recompose a 16-tile atlas every frame. Gating on
  the _tile_ rect means a rebuild happens roughly once per tile-width of rotation — about every
  2.8 s for Mars at level 4, and never while parked at a slow or tidally-locked body.
- **The atlas rect is deliberately not wrapped into [0,1).** The shader's subtract-and-divide needs
  `u1 > u0` even when the patch straddles the antimeridian; wrapping would invert the rect and
  mirror the atlas. Pinned by a test.

## Part 2 — Venus, and the honesty problem that was not where I expected

The owner's call was to make the radar map's contradiction the feature: descend through the real
cloud deck and emerge over the Magellan surface, which is honest because radar-through-cloud is
exactly how humanity has actually seen that surface.

Astra's brief then relocated the risk. **The dishonesty is not the radar map — it is lighting it.**
C4.1's directional Sun, Lunar-Lambert reflectance and height-derived normals applied to Magellan
data would manufacture geometry that is not there and then cast convincing light across it, and it
would pass every review because it would look superb. Radar brightness is roughness and slope, not
reflectance.

So below ~45 km the body takes a **shader fork**: no directional term, no terminator, no normal
perturbation — just the real Venera illuminant at the real relative illuminance. That is also
simply what is true: Venera 9/10/13/14 recorded flat, shadowless orange light with no solar disc.

**Two asset facts, re-measured this session rather than taken on trust:**

- `venus-cloud.jpg` has **identical statistics in all three channels** (mean 171.4, σ 35.1) — one
  band replicated, i.e. a UV image — and **20.5% contrast against a real visible-light 1–3%**.
  Shipping it as-is would present ultraviolet structure as a photograph. Flattened to ~2.5%.
- `venus-ultra.jpg`'s orange cast **is** the real Venera illuminant (1.00, 0.57, 0.20), which Astra
  derived independently from Rayleigh transmission and matched to two decimals. The tint was never
  the problem; only the spatial contrast was.

**Real numbers drive the descent**, not invented keyframes: entry above the 70 km cloud top,
breakout at the real 47.5 km cloud base, ending at 20 km — Astra: **do not land**, because at the
surface visibility is 3.3 km and the view from just under the deck is the actual payoff. Half the
20 s runtime is spent inside the deck, where the structure is. Super-rotation advects at **real
time 1.0×**: on the 1e3 rotation clock the real 100 m/s cloud-top wind would become 100 km/s, or
0.033 c.

**Declared, because it cannot be otherwise:** the descent is played as a _parameter_, not a camera
translation. At `PLANET_SPHERE_RADIUS = 26` the entire 70 km deck is **0.097 world units** thick, so
a literally-scaled descent would move the camera a tenth of a unit and put both shells inside each
other's depth precision. The altitudes and their ordering are real; only their mapping to world
units is declared.

## Part 3 — A reserved-identifier bug, from the side I was watching least

The GLSL twin named the fork's result `flat`. **`flat` is a reserved interpolation qualifier in
GLSL ES 3.0**, so the shader silently failed to compile — and the only symptom was
`planetSphereReady` staying false, with **no console error to point at it**.

This is TR-045's lesson (reserved identifiers blank the scene) arriving from the **GLSL** side
rather than the WGSL side. What makes it worth recording: I had already noticed the hazard for
WGSL, named that twin `flatLit`, and written a comment about it — then left the GLSL twin as
`flat`. The twins disagreed, and only one of them broke. The E2E caught it because it asserts
material readiness rather than mere existence; a "does the sphere exist" test would have passed.

Both twins are now `flatLit`, and a unit test pins that `vec3 flat =` cannot come back.

## Part 6 — ADDENDUM (same day): the browser-pane failure is diagnosed, and most of this session's work is now confirmed live

TR-075, TR-076 and TR-077 each recorded "the embedded browser pane failed to drive this scene"
without establishing why. Retried on owner request, and the cause is now measured rather than
guessed.

**The pane's page is never visible.** `document.hidden === true`, `visibilityState === "hidden"`,
and a direct probe recorded **0 `requestAnimationFrame` callbacks in 1 second**. Every symptom
across four sessions follows from that single fact:

| Symptom (4 sessions)             | Cause                                                                        |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `arrivedId` never advanced       | warp progress is integrated per rendered frame; the render loop never ticked |
| Bulk layers never loaded         | they are triggered from the **first rendered frame**                         |
| `renderFrames` absent from stats | the loop had genuinely never run                                             |
| Screenshots time out             | the compositor has no painted frame to capture                               |

**This is an instrument limitation, not a product defect.** In a real, visible browser rAF fires
normally — which is exactly what the real-Chromium E2E suite has been demonstrating all along.

### Driving it anyway

Two workarounds made the scene fully exercisable, and both are legitimate for verification because
they drive the engine's own real code paths rather than substituting for them:

1. **Pump the registered render loop on a timer** — `engine._activeRenderLoops.forEach(f => f())`
   under `setInterval`, since `setTimeout` is throttled for a hidden page but not silenced the way
   rAF is. Warp immediately completed and `arrivedId` reached `"mars"`.
2. **Shim `requestAnimationFrame` onto `setTimeout`**, needed because some waiters recurse through
   rAF directly (see the finding below).

### Verified live, in a real browser, on WebGPU

### One real finding: `_waitForWarpIdle`'s timeout is ineffective when rAF is dead

```ts
const check = () => {
  if (this.warp.mode === "idle" || performance.now() - start > maxWaitMs) {
    resolve();
    return;
  }
  requestAnimationFrame(check); // <-- the only thing that re-runs the timeout check
};
```

The `maxWaitMs = 8000` guard is only evaluated _inside_ the rAF callback, so if rAF never fires the
guard never fires either and the promise hangs forever. That is what parked the bulk layers until
the shim went in.

**Not a shipping defect**, and deliberately not "fixed" in this session: in a real browser rAF stops
only while a tab is backgrounded, and it resumes on focus, so the wait self-heals and the behaviour
(don't build 3.6M-vertex geometry for a tab nobody is looking at) is arguably correct. But the
timeout reads as a safety net and is not one, which is worth knowing before someone relies on it.
Recorded here rather than silently patched, because changing a load-gating path deserves its own
change with its own verification.

### What this addendum does NOT establish

**Still no pixels.** Screenshots time out even with frames being produced, because the pane does not
paint. Canvas readback was deliberately not attempted: CLAUDE.md non-negotiable #11 states a WebGPU
canvas reads back transparent regardless of correctness, so it would have produced a confident
false negative.

So every "owner-eyes outstanding" item stands unchanged — this addendum upgrades them from _"the
feature may not even run"_ to **"the feature demonstrably runs; how it looks is unverified"**, which
is a materially different and much smaller gap.

  real-device data first.

**Owner-eyes outstanding**, and larger than usual this time because three features landed
unobserved: whether streamed detail visibly sharpens Mars and the Moon over the ultra map alone;
whether the Venus descent reads as a descent at 20 s; and whether the flat-lit radar surface at the

scene four sessions running, so none of it was checked by eye here.
